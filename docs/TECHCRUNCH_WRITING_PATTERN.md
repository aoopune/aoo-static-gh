# TechCrunch writing pattern

**Locked reference.** Fundamental title and body patterns observed across TechCrunch. No article-specific facts — structure and decision logic only.

- Pass 1: [techcrunch.com/latest](https://techcrunch.com/latest/) (Aug 2026)

## Title patterns

Each pattern: **why** (2 sentences) + **what makes it** (2 sentences).

### 1. Straight news headline

**Why:** The reader must get the full fact from the title alone because search, feeds, and mobile skimming leave no room for a tease. Specificity signals credibility before the first click.

**What makes it:** Actor + strong verb + concrete detail (number, place, product, outcome). Usually 8–15 words; no hype adjectives; rarely starts with “In a move to…”

### 2. Angle / interpretation headline

**Why:** A flat announcement headline is interchangeable with every other outlet; the angle is the reason to read TechCrunch instead of the press release. Skepticism, rivalry, or “what they’re not saying” earns attention from insiders.

**What makes it:** Same underlying event as news, but the title adds meaning — debunk, one-up, capitalize-on, didn’t really, as long as. The title promises a story behind the announcement, not just the announcement.

### 3. Question or provocation

**Why:** A question or blunt contradiction stops the scroll when the stakes are cultural or moral, not purely corporate. It signals the piece has a point of view beyond reporting.

**What makes it:** Opens with doubt, surprise, or tension; often a question mark or an em dash to a punchy second clause. Body must answer or justify the provocation — title and body are a matched pair.

### 4. Promise headline (“Here’s why” / “secret weapon” / “how X works”)

**Why:** The reader is buying insight, not inventory; the title contracts to explain a mechanism or hidden advantage. Works for explainers and analysis where the lede alone isn’t enough.

**What makes it:** Event or topic + explicit promise of explanation (Here’s why, secret weapon, how X will work). Body must deliver the mechanism — mismatch breaks trust.

### 5. “Report says” / In Brief

**Why:** Speed beats depth when the story is small, early, or sourced from another outlet before full verification. Flagging uncertainty (“report says”, “reportedly”) protects credibility while still being first.

**What makes it:** Shorter title, often funding or scoop-shaped; attribution to Bloomberg/Reuters/etc. in title or first line. Body stays 150–300 words — no faux depth.

### 6. Direct reader instruction

**Why:** On security and safety stories, the title’s job is utility, not curiosity — people share and act on “do this now” more than “something happened.” It signals the piece will tell them what to do, not just what occurred.

**What makes it:** If/when + condition + imperative (take it seriously, enable X). Body must give concrete steps before the close — the title is a contract for action.

### 7. Quoted speech in title

**Why:** One vivid phrase from a person or document feels human and specific in a feed of generic corporate language. It signals the piece has voice and a focal detail worth reading for.

**What makes it:** Single quoted phrase that carries the story (CEO line, vivid action, coined fear-name). The quote is the hook; the rest of the title supplies who and context.

### 8. Reversal / turnaround

**Why:** The first clause sets a popular belief; the second flips it. That tension is the click — the reader wants to see whether the flip is earned.

**What makes it:** Two-beat structure: claim or crowd reaction, then reversal (Until it doesn’t / Sorry, haters / doing just fine). Body must open on the belief, then deliver the contradictory evidence.

### 9. How-to guide

**Why:** Distinct from a one-line instruction: the reader wants a full procedure they can follow, not a news fact. Evergreen utility drives search and saves.

**What makes it:** Opens with “How to…” or equivalent procedural promise. Body is step-by-step with H2s per platform/product and exact click paths — not narrative news.

### 10. Aggregator / exhaustive inventory

**Why:** The value is completeness, not a single story — “every X that meets Y” is a reference piece people bookmark and return to. Thresholds (over $100M) make the list feel finite and fair.

**What makes it:** “Every [category] that [threshold].” Body is entry-by-entry (company + raise + investors), updated over time — not a short product roundup.

### 11. “What we know” status roundup

**Why:** When a story is messy, partial, and still developing, the honest sell is clarity about known vs unknown — not a false definitive headline. Readers click to get oriented amid conflicting claims.

**What makes it:** Title framed as “What we know about…” Body uses FAQ-style H2s (Where? Who? What effects?) and states uncertainty plainly when attribution is incomplete.

### 12. Two-sentence narrative

**Why:** A mini-story in the title (setup → consequence) creates curiosity without a question mark. It works when the experiment or scene is more interesting than the company announcement.

**What makes it:** Sentence one sets the action; sentence two delivers the twist or result. Body opens with the scene, then expands evidence and stakes.

### 13. Expert explains

**Why:** Named authority in the title signals insider access and transfers trust from the person to the piece. Works when the subject is jargon-heavy and needs a guide, not a scoop.

**What makes it:** Named expert + “explains why / how.” Body is interview-driven: concept taught through their quotes, not a press-release rewrite.

### 14. First-person reporter stance

**Why:** The reporter’s doubt becomes the hook — “and I have questions” signals skepticism the reader is invited to share. Used when the company’s framing smells off and the desk won’t play along.

**What makes it:** News clause + first-person skepticism (“again, and I have questions”). Body contrasts filings, timelines, and prior claims; ends without resolving into company spin.

## Body patterns

Each pattern: **why** (2 sentences) + **what makes it** (2 sentences).

### 1. The lede (first paragraph)

**Why:** The audience skims; if paragraph one doesn’t land who, what, and why it matters, they leave. No throat-clearing — respect for reader time is part of the brand.

**What makes it:** One block: fact first, source or timing early, one line of stakes. Never “In today’s fast-paced world” or background before the news.

### 2. Scale / numbers block

**Why:** Insiders trust numbers and comparison more than adjectives; scale and money answer “how big is this really?” faster than narrative. History (“last time”, prior round, prior incident) anchors surprise.

**What makes it:** Deal size, user/victim counts, valuation steps, named rivals or precedents in paragraphs 2–4. Often a pivot: “But the question is…” — news delivered, real story starts.

### 3. “So what” middle

**Why:** The product launch isn’t the story; placement in the market, conflict, risk, and what’s still unknown are. This block is why the piece isn’t a rewritten press release.

**What makes it:** Industry context, competitor list, expert or analyst quote, explicit “did not respond”, downside or prior failures. Attribution on every non-obvious claim.

### 4. Voice by beat

**Why:** Tone must match topic expectation — sober for breaches and legal probes, explanatory for complex tech, sharp for consumer harm and absurd corporate spin. Wrong voice reads as untrustworthy or fluffy.

**What makes it:** Neutral short sentences (security, funding, transport); one taught concept per explainer (climate, policy, AI mechanics); allowed opinion/sarcasm only when human stakes are clear (culture, forced features, harm to vulnerable groups).

### 5. Format choice

**Why:** Effort must match urgency — don’t write 800 words to flag a scoop, don’t write 200 when the reader needs a taught concept. Format signals how much the desk invested.

**What makes it:** Full piece (400–900+ words) for exclusives, analysis, cultural takes; In Brief for fast funding/report-follows; listicle (H2 + price + one paragraph) for evergreen product guides; how-to / inventory / status roundup when those title types are used.

### 6. The close

**Why:** Insiders already got the lede; ending on recap wastes the last screen. Open threads, pending answers, or one practical line match how news actually works — stories don’t always resolve.

**What makes it:** Ends on unknown (postmortem pending, no comment), forward look (rollout cities, next round), or actionable advice (security only — e.g. enable Lockdown Mode). No marketing CTA, no summary paragraph repeating the lede.

### 7. Character / scene lede

**Why:** For culture and venture features, opening on a person makes the pressure feel real before the thesis arrives. The news is the pattern; the person is the proof.

**What makes it:** First paragraphs follow one founder/user with age, stakes, and a vivid detail; thesis and investor quotes arrive after the scene is set. Used for features — not for breaking news.

### 8. Step-by-step utility body

**Why:** A how-to title fails if the body narrates instead of instructs. Readers need click paths they can follow without leaving the page.

**What makes it:** H2 per platform or tool; numbered or sequential actions (“click Settings → Security”); screenshots or exact UI labels; short safety preamble (password manager, MFA) before the steps.

### 9. Entry-by-entry inventory body

**Why:** An aggregator title promises a complete set, so the body must be scannable reference entries, not one continuous essay. People return to update and compare.

**What makes it:** One block per company/item (name, total raised, latest round, key investors); ordered by scale or alphabet; updated note when the list is refreshed.

### 10. Known vs unknown FAQ body

**Why:** Developing stories spawn conflicting claims; organizing by reader questions prevents burying uncertainty. Honesty about what isn’t confirmed builds more trust than a premature definitive take.

**What makes it:** H2s as questions (Where? Who? What effects?); each section stacks attributed claims and ends with what remains unknown. No forced conclusion when attribution is incomplete.

### 11. Rebuttal arc

**Why:** When the title flips popular belief, the body must stage the belief first, then dismantle it with evidence — otherwise the turnaround feels cheap. The emotional payoff is “the crowd was wrong.”

**What makes it:** Open on the hate/hype/conventional wisdom; mid-piece delivers numbers or outcomes that contradict it; close on why the flip was predictable in hindsight. Matches reversal titles.

## Title ↔ body contract

| Title type | Body must deliver |
|------------|-------------------|
| Straight news | Lede matches title fact-for-fact; depth in middle |
| Angle / interpretation | Evidence for the angle in first half; not just PR |
| Question / provocation | Answer or justify by end; stance clear |
| Promise (“Here’s why”) | Mechanism or explanation, not more announcement |
| “Report says” / In Brief | Short; caveats early; no padded analysis |
| Reader instruction | Concrete steps; actionable close |
| Quoted speech | The quote’s context and meaning unpacked in body |
| Reversal / turnaround | Belief first, then contradicting evidence |
| How-to guide | Step-by-step click paths; H2 per tool |
| Aggregator / inventory | Entry-by-entry reference blocks |
| “What we know” | FAQ sections; known vs unknown stated plainly |
| Two-sentence narrative | Scene/setup early; twist explained with evidence |
| Expert explains | Interview-driven teaching through named authority |
| First-person stance | Reporter skepticism backed by filings/timeline gaps |

## One-line cheat sheet

| Part | Why | What makes it |
|------|-----|----------------|
| **Title** | Earn attention in one line on search and social | Actor + verb + specific detail (+ optional angle, flip, promise, or utility) |
| **Body** | Deliver the story behind the announcement | Fact → numbers → meaning → voice matched to beat → no bow |

## What they avoid

- Press-release voice (“excited to announce”) in body or title
- Burying the news below context
- Unattributed claims (use “report says”, named outlet, or “sources familiar”)
- Generic superlatives (revolutionary, game-changing)
- False balance when harm is clear
- Ending with “learn more at…” or a recap paragraph
- Promise / how-to / reversal titles that the body does not earn
