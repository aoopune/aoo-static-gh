# Low cognitive-load writing principles

Source boil-downs from research on writing that reduces context-switching, decoding cost, subject overload, needless length, and working-memory strain.

**How to use this file:** For each topic, read the **1-line core** first. Use the **fundamental gist** when drafting or revising. The links are sources, not required reading every time.

**Master rule for our texts:** Write so the reader’s next bit of sense arrives where the brain already expects it — one clear actor doing one clear thing, known before new, short when short is enough — so meaning is received, not decoded.

## 1. Cheung — Plain language to minimize cognitive load

**Source:** [IEEE / DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/tpc.2017.2759639) · [PDF](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/18960/cheung-ieee.pdf)

**1-line core:** Hard writing steals working memory; plain language returns it so understanding can happen.

**Fundamental gist:**
Working memory can hold only a few items for a few seconds; when a text’s demand exceeds that limit, learning and understanding fail. Cognitive load splits into what the topic truly needs (intrinsic), what bad design adds for no reason (extraneous), and what helps the reader build lasting understanding (germane). Plain language’s job is to strip extraneous load — unclear structure, needless complexity, late main points — so readers can spend scarce attention on the real content. Cheung’s deeper claim is ethical as well as technical: people under high life stress already have less free working memory, so writing that is harder than it must be widens inequity; writing that is plain enough for System-1-easy reading restores access for everyone.

## 2. University Affairs — Reducing the weight of your words (Cheung applied)

**Source:** [Reducing the weight of your words](https://universityaffairs.ca/career-advice/reducing-the-weight-of-your-words/)

**1-line core:** Splitting subject from verb forces the reader to hold unfinished meaning until the sentence ends.

**Fundamental gist:**
English readers expect the subject and the verb to arrive close together near the start so they can resolve “who is doing what” immediately. When many words sit between them, the reader must store the subject, store the intervening material, and stay alert for the missing verb — all of that is extraneous load. Closing the subject–verb gap lets the brain finish one unit of meaning and free working memory for what comes next. The article’s practical truth is simple: clarity is not “dumbing down”; it is refusing to make the reader do unpaid structural labor.

## 3. AJE — Three cognitive learning principles for clarity

**Source:** [Improve Your Writing Clarity…](https://blog.aje.com/en/improve-your-writing-clarity-through-these-three-cognitive-learning-principles)

**1-line core:** One idea, no dead words, and old→new structure cut load and match how readers expect sentences to work.

**Fundamental gist:**
First principle (cognitive load): put only one idea in a sentence; if you can delete words without changing meaning, delete them. Second principle (reader bias / emphasis): the end of a sentence is where readers put stress, so put the important new claim there. Third principle (reader expectations): open with the topic the sentence is about (often familiar), then move to what is new, so the next sentence can pick up that new as its known start. Together these say: reduce what the mind must juggle, place emphasis where emphasis already lands, and chain sentences so each beginning answers what the last ending set up.

## 4. ReadabilityFormulas — Cognitive Load Theory for writing style

**Source:** [Improve Your Writing Style with Cognitive Load Theory](https://readabilityformulas.com/improve-your-writing-style-with-cognitive-load-theory/)

**1-line core:** Every sentence can add intrinsic, extraneous, or useful load; cut the useless kind.

**Fundamental gist:**
John Sweller’s Cognitive Load Theory says working memory is the bottleneck: new text must be processed there before it can become lasting knowledge. Intrinsic load is the real difficulty of the idea; germane load is the healthy work of connecting and remembering; extraneous load is friction from jargon, clutter, and poor structure. A short sentence can still create many decoding steps if it forces re-reads, ambiguous references, or hidden actors. The writer’s job is not to remove all mental effort, but to remove effort that does not buy understanding.

## 5. Gopen & Swan — The Science of Scientific Writing

**Source:** [American Scientist](https://www.americanscientist.org/blog/the-long-view/the-science-of-scientific-writing) · [PDF](https://course.ccs.neu.edu/cs7976f19/Materials/Gopen-Swan%201990.pdf)

**1-line core:** Meaning lands when information sits where readers already look for it.

**Fundamental gist:**
Communication succeeds only if most readers perceive what the writer meant; structure is the main signal that tells them how to interpret substance. Readers expect the grammatical subject to meet its verb quickly; long interruptions between them are read as asides even when they contain the real point. The beginning of a sentence (topic position) should give perspective, linkage, and familiar “old” information; the end (stress position) should carry the new information worth emphasis. When writers violate these slots, readers divert energy from content to unraveling structure — and as context gets denser, misreading rises fast. Clarification is not the same as simplification: keep the hard ideas, put them where the reading brain expects them.

**Structural principles (use as rules):**
1. Follow a subject as soon as possible with its verb.
2. Put the new information you want emphasized in the stress position (end).
3. Put the person/thing whose story the sentence tells in the topic position (start).
4. Put appropriate old information in the topic position to link backward.
5. Provide context before asking the reader to consider anything new.

## 6. Williams — Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace

**Source:** Overviews — [notes](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/) · [essay](https://experiencemachines.substack.com/p/the-best-book-on-writing-is-style)

**1-line core:** Clear prose is characters doing actions, strung old-to-new so the story stays trackable.

**Fundamental gist:**
A sentence is a miniature story; readers feel clarity when grammatical subjects name the characters and verbs name the actions those characters perform. Unclear writing often hides the action inside nouns (nominalizations: “decision,” “implementation”) and hides or scatters the people who act. Cohesion comes from beginning sentences with familiar information and ending with new information so each sentence hands the next a ready starting point. Emphasis follows the same architecture: stress what matters at points of closure. The book’s ethical edge: writers who bury actors and actions force readers to reconstruct the story; clear style is a responsibility to the reader’s time and mind.

## 7. Given–New principle (Haviland & Clark)

**Source:** [1974 paper PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Haviland,%20S.E.%20_%20Clark,%20H.H.%20_What's%20new_%20Acquiring%20new%20information%20as%20a%20process%20in%20comprehension_%201974.pdf) · [Rochester teaching note](https://writing.rochester.edu/faculty/wsap/resources/given-new/)

**1-line core:** Readers attach new facts to what they already hold; put known first or they must rebuild.

**Fundamental gist:**
Comprehension is not absorbing a whole sentence as one blob; listeners/readers split Given (already assumed) from New (to be added). The Given acts like an address into memory: find the matching antecedent, then attach the New to it. When the Given has no clear antecedent, the reader must build a bridge, treat everything as new, or recompute what was Given — all of which costs time and raises error. Ease of understanding tracks how easily the Given points to something already in mind and how cleanly the New can dock there. Writing that fronts brand-new crowds of subjects before any anchor forces exactly that costly rebuild.

## 8. Psychology Today — Science-backed ways to write clearly

**Source:** [5 Science-Backed Ways to Write Clearly](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/how-writing-works/202406/5-science-backed-ways-to-write-clearly)

**1-line core:** Brains parse “who did what to whom” fast; fuzzy actors and distant verbs slow that parse.

**Fundamental gist:**
Readers experience less cognitive load when a sentence maps cleanly onto cause and effect — who did what to whom. Active voice and noun subjects that can actually act (people, organizations, concrete things) are processed with more speed and less effort than vague or passive setups. Pronouns as subjects, especially this/that/these/those/it, often save the writer effort while sending the reader backward to recover referents, which fights the brain’s forward-prediction mode. Action verbs enrich meaning and memory more than abstract non-action verbs. Distance between subject and verb increases both time and error in identifying the core relation — so proximity is not style polish; it is how the parse stays cheap.

## 9. Cambridge — The science behind writing with clarity

**Source:** [The Science behind Writing with Clarity](https://cambridgeblog.org/2025/01/the-science-behind-writing-with-clarity/)

**1-line core:** Same brain rules as above: concrete actors, close verbs, no lazy pronouns that push work onto the reader.

**Fundamental gist:**
This piece restates the neuroscience/clarity line for a wider audience: clarity is alignment with how reading brains extract meaning, not a taste for “simple vibes.” Sentences that present capable actors and clear cause–effect cost less effort; pronoun-led openings often do the opposite. Writers can be grammatically correct and still inflict unnecessary decoding work. The fundamental truth shared with the Psychology Today piece: if a rewrite makes the same claim with a clearer actor and a nearer verb, the reader’s brain finishes sooner and with fewer mistakes — that is the win, not sounding plainer for its own sake.

## 10. Dependency length minimization

**Source:** [Futrell et al., PNAS](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Futrell_et_al_2015_PNAS.pdf) · [Annual Review overview](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-linguistics-011817-045617)

**1-line core:** Related words far apart cost more memory; keep what belongs together near each other.

**Fundamental gist:**
A sentence is a web of head–modifier dependencies (who goes with which verb, which adjective with which noun, and so on). Models of production and comprehension predict that long dependencies are hard: the mind must keep an open relation unresolved across intervening words. Across languages and speaker choices, people tend to prefer orders that shorten those distances. For writing practice, the usable truth is: do not strand a verb far from its subject, or a modifier far from what it modifies, when a nearer arrangement says the same thing. Short dependency length is a cognitive efficiency principle, not an aesthetic preference.

## 11. PLOS One — Syntactic complexity vs reading time

**Source:** [Comparing predictors of sentence self-paced reading times](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0254546)

**1-line core:** Heavier syntax and harder word sequences make reading slower even when content is the same.

**Fundamental gist:**
Reading a sentence is many small processes aiming at one coherent interpretation; complexity slows that journey. This study finds that both syntactic complexity measures and transitional-probability (how expected the next words are) independently predict reading times. Word length, frequency, and position still explain a lot, but structure and predictability matter on top of that. The writing takeaway is empirical, not moralistic: when you pack more clause-work and less-expected sequences into a line, readers pay in milliseconds — and those milliseconds add up into fatigue, rereads, and drop-off. Prefer predictable, lighter structures when they still carry the meaning.

## 12. Federal Plain Language Guidelines

**Source:** [Digital.gov Clear and short](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short) · [Guidelines PDF](https://centerforplainlanguage.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf)

**1-line core:** Government rulebook: short, one idea, subject–verb–object close, main point before exceptions.

**Fundamental gist:**
These guidelines treat complexity as the enemy of clear public communication: long sentences stuffed with clauses and exceptions bury the point. Express one idea per sentence; break complex relations into separate sentences that still show the link (for example, “suppose… / in this case…”). Keep subject, verb, and object close; avoid double negatives and nested exceptions; put the main idea before conditions. At paragraph level, one topic per paragraph, short blocks, clear topic sentences and transitions. The fundamental stance: if the audience cannot find, understand, and use the information on first pass, the writing failed — regardless of how complete it looked to the writer.

## 13. Digital.gov / VA — Short sentences in practice

**Source:** [VA: Use short sentences](https://design.va.gov/content-style-guide/plain-language/use-short-sentences)

**1-line core:** Cap length and split ideas so each sentence has one job the reader can finish cleanly.

**Fundamental gist:**
Operational plain language often targets roughly one subject and verb per sentence and a soft ceiling around 25 words (federal averages often aim ~15–20). Lists and even starting with “and/or/so/but” are allowed when they prevent a overloaded single line. Splitting is not chopping meaning; it is giving each claim a finish line so the reader can check understanding before the next claim. The fundamental truth: length is a proxy for load — when a sentence tries to do two jobs, make two sentences.

## 14. UNC — Minimized Text Complexity Guidelines

**Source:** [PDF](https://www.med.unc.edu/healthsciences/clds/wp-content/uploads/sites/859/2021/06/Minimized-Text-Complexity-Guidelines-version-2.03.2021.pdf)

**1-line core:** Clinical/accessibility rules that shrink sentence load so meaning survives limited literacy and attention.

**Fundamental gist:**
These guidelines come from health/communication disability work: keep documents focused, short, and cohesive so readers are not flooded. At sentence level: one key point per sentence, short word counts, complex sentences only when the relation itself requires them, and prefer repeating clear patterns over stylish variation. Avoid negation where a positive statement works; maximize cohesion by repeating content words across sentences so the thread stays visible. When a new sentence does not relate to the prior one, start a new paragraph. The fundamental truth: minimized complexity is design for constrained working memory — not “talking down,” but refusing to waste scarce attention.

## 15. Orwell — Politics and the English Language

**Source:** [Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)

**1-line core:** Prefer the short true wording; cut padding that hides meaning behind impressive fog.

**Fundamental gist:**
Orwell argues bad modern prose often chooses ready-made phrases over exact meaning, which blurs thought as well as language. A careful writer asks what they are trying to say, which words and images make it clearer, whether it can be shorter, and whether anything is avoidably ugly. His rules: no stale figures of speech; no long word where a short one works; cut any word you can; prefer active to passive; prefer everyday English to jargon or foreign garnish; break any rule rather than write something barbarous. The fundamental truth is not “always simple”: it is let meaning choose the words, and refuse verbal fog that costs the reader decoding without buying truth.

**Orwell’s six rules (compact):**
1. Never use a metaphor/simile/figure you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign, scientific, or jargon word if an everyday equivalent exists.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

## 16. Active vs passive — comprehension research synthesis

**Source:** Example review framing — active generally aids comprehension in academic texts (see experimental comparisons summarized in active/passive comprehension literature; e.g. discussions tied to clarity guidelines)

**1-line core:** Active usually makes the actor and action clearer and faster to understand.

**Fundamental gist:**
Across many readability and comprehension comparisons, active voice tends to produce faster processing, clearer responsibility, and higher comprehension than default passive. Passive is not forbidden: it is useful when the patient/object is the continuing topic or the actor is unknown or rightly de-emphasized. The failure mode is habitual passive that hides who did what, adding words and ambiguity for no rhetorical gain. Fundamental rule: choose active unless you have a specific reason the sentence’s story is about the thing acted upon.

## 17. Simplifying complex sentences (practice guides)

**Source:** e.g. [Adobe: How to simplify complex sentences](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/resources/how-to-simplify-complex-sentences.html)

**1-line core:** Complexity is fine only when it carries needed relation; otherwise split and strip.

**Fundamental gist:**
Complex sentences are tools for real causal, conditional, or hierarchical relations — not a default prestige style. Difficulty comes from stacked clauses, filler phrases, buried subjects, jargon, and passives that obscure the actor. Simplification means keep the meaning, remove obstacles: delete redundancy, prefer common words, split overloaded lines, move modifiers so they do not delay the core clause. Read aloud as a check: if you lose the thread mid-sentence, the reader will too. Fundamental truth: spend complexity on relationships that matter; never on decoration.

## Unified writing principles (use this checklist)

Derived only from the fundamentals above — for drafting and revising our texts.

1. **One idea per sentence** unless a single relation truly requires joining.
2. **Name the character; name the action** — subject = who/what the sentence is about; verb = what they do.
3. **Keep subject and verb close** — no long middle that the reader must hold open.
4. **Keep related words near** — modifiers next to what they modify; short dependencies.
5. **Old before new** — start from what the reader already has; end on what is new and important.
6. **Main point before exceptions** — do not open with conditions that hide the claim.
7. **Cut words that do not change meaning** — shorter only when meaning is intact.
8. **Prefer short everyday words** when they carry the same sense.
9. **Prefer active voice** unless the story is rightly about the thing acted on.
10. **Do not open on vague this/that/it** unless the referent is unmistakable without a backward search.
11. **Do not introduce a crowd of new subjects** before the reader has an anchor.
12. **Repeat key content words** across sentences when needed for cohesion — stylish synonym-hopping can break the thread.
13. **Split when the reader would have to re-read** — two clear sentences beat one clever maze.
14. **Spend complexity only on real relations** — cause, condition, contrast — never on prestige.
15. **Success test:** Can the intended reader find, understand, and use the point on the first pass without holding unfinished structure in memory?
