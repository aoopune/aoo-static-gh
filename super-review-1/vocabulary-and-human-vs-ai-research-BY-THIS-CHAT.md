# Vocabulary limits and human vs AI voice — research notes

**Purpose:** Plain-language notes from this session’s research. Each item states the idea in simple words, then the **fundamental truth** — why it works that way at the root.

**How to use:** Skim **Quick stacks** first. When writing or reviewing copy, open the section that matches your question.

**Location:** `super-review-1/vocabulary-and-human-vs-ai-research-BY-THIS-CHAT.md`

---

## Quick stack — vocabulary (one pass)

1. A small set of common words does most of daily talk — not because people are lazy, but because word use follows a steep curve (Zipf).
2. There is no one magic “max words for everything” — the number depends on the job (chat, TV, reading, specialist topics).
3. You can *rephrase* almost any idea with a tiny word list (Ogden’s 850, Munroe’s 1,000) — but it gets long and awkward.
4. People *know* far more words than they *use* in a normal day.
5. For clear writing (including guides), favour high-frequency words — but specialist topics still need some precise terms.

---

## Quick stack — human vs AI voice (one pass)

1. AI picks the most *likely* next word; humans pick words for memory, mood, and who they’re talking to.
2. AI text is often smooth, even, and complete; human text is often uneven, specific, and selective.
3. One fancy word does not prove AI — **clusters** of patterns do.
4. Most people cannot reliably spot AI writing (~50–59% accuracy); computers can (~85–96%) because they measure patterns at scale.
5. Human-feeling copy = concrete details + a point of view + uneven rhythm + no empty templates.

# Part A — How many words for daily life?

---

## A1 — There is no single “max words” article

**Simple words:** No one study says “use exactly X words and never learn another.” Research gives **ranges for different jobs** — talking, watching TV, reading books.

**Fundamental truth:** Language is a tool for many jobs. “Enough words” depends on what you’re trying to do. A number that covers small talk will not cover medical papers. Researchers measure **coverage** (how much you understand or can say), not a fixed ceiling for all of life.

**Sources:** [BBC — How many words?](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-44569277) · [Nation (2006)](https://lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)

---

## A2 — Zipf’s law: a few words do most of the work

**Simple words:** In every language, a handful of words appear constantly; thousands of others appear almost never. The top 1,000 words cover roughly **75–80%** of normal speech.

**Fundamental truth:** Word use is not flat — it is heavily skewed. The most common word (“the”) alone can be ~5–7% of everything written. Each step down the frequency list adds less new coverage. That is why learning the **right** 1,000 words pays off more than learning 1,000 random words. The curve is a property of how humans actually communicate, not a rule someone invented.

**Sources:** [Zipf review — PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4176592/) · [Nation (2006)](https://lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)

---

## A3 — ~800–1,000 words for basic day-to-day talk

**Simple words:** Linguist Paul Nation (via BBC) suggests **800–1,000 common word families** for basic daily conversation — enough to understand about **75%** of normal spoken English.

**Fundamental truth:** Daily life repeats the same situations (food, home, work, money, travel). Those situations reuse the same core words. You do not need “abode” if “house” works. The threshold is about **functional coverage in familiar settings**, not expressing every nuance or every specialist idea.

**Sources:** [BBC — Paul Nation](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-44569277)

---

## A4 — ~2,000–3,000 words for comfortable conversation

**Simple words:** To follow most everyday talk and TV without strain, research often cites **2,000–3,000 word families** (~85–95% coverage of casual speech).

**Fundamental truth:** Below ~95% word coverage, unknown words appear often enough that the brain must guess from context — doable, but tiring. Comfortable conversation means you are not constantly filling gaps. Context helps, but there is a point where too many gaps breaks the thread. That point sits around a few thousand high-frequency families for speech.

**Sources:** [Nation (2006)](https://lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf) · [TalkToDia summary](https://talktodia.com/en/blog/first-1000-words-cover-75-percent)

---

## A5 — ~6,000–9,000 words for reading without strain

**Simple words:** To read newspapers, blogs, or novels with little help, research suggests **6,000–7,000** families for spoken text and **8,000–9,000** for written text (~98% coverage).

**Fundamental truth:** Written English uses more low-frequency words than speech. Books and articles assume a wider vocabulary than chat. The 98% threshold matters because below that, rare words appear often enough to interrupt flow. Reading is harder than listening because you cannot ask the writer to repeat themselves.

**Sources:** [Nation (2006)](https://lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)

---

## A6 — Basic English: 850 words to “say almost everything”

**Simple words:** In the 1930s, C.K. Ogden proposed **850 core English words** that could express what people normally say with **15,000–20,000** — by paraphrasing (“have a meal” instead of “eat”).

**Fundamental truth:** Vocabulary size and **expressive power** are not the same thing. With enough paraphrase and a small set of “operator” verbs (get, make, have, do), you can route around missing words. Ogden’s claim is about **rephrasing capacity**, not that people naturally stop at 850. The trade-off is length and naturalness — the idea works, but it often sounds stiff.

**Sources:** [Ogden — Basic English, 1937](https://zbenglish.net/sites/basic/begr.html) · [Wikipedia — Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English) · [The Week summary](https://theweek.com/articles/560685/does-english-only-need-850-words-guy-thought)

---

## A7 — Thing Explainer: 1,000 most common words for hard topics

**Simple words:** Randall Munroe (xkcd) showed you can explain rockets, cells, and nuclear plants using only the **1,000 most common English words** — “up-goer” for rocket, “food-heating radio box” for microwave.

**Fundamental truth:** Complex ideas can be broken into simpler parts and described with common words — if you accept longer, odder phrasing. Munroe’s project is proof of **paraphrase depth**, not that 1,000 words is the best daily toolkit. It shows the gap between “can be said” and “can be said well.”

**Sources:** [xkcd — Up Goer Five](https://xkcd.com/1133/) · [Thing Explainer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thing_Explainer) · [arXiv CNL paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1605.02457)

---

## A8 — You know many more words than you use daily

**Simple words:** An average adult **knows** roughly **20,000–35,000 word families** (or more by some counts), but **uses** a much smaller set in normal conversation — often cited around **1,500–3,000** active words.

**Fundamental truth:** Vocabulary has two layers: **passive** (recognise when reading) and **active** (use when speaking or writing). Daily life draws from the active core. The rest is for reading, specialist work, precision, and nuance. Knowing a word and needing it today are different things.

**Sources:** [Brysbaert et al. (2016) — Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full) · [Goulden, Nation & Read (1990)](https://vocabulary-test.com/native-speaker-vocabulary/)

---

## A9 — “Never need new words” is not true for specialist life

**Simple words:** For normal chat, a small core is enough. For law, medicine, finance, hobbies, or art, you need **extra words** — or you spend many sentences paraphrasing one precise term.

**Fundamental truth:** Precision has a cost. Either you learn the term (“EMI,” “deductible,” “leverage”) or you pay with length and ambiguity. Paraphrase always works in theory; in practice, specialist domains optimise for short, exact labels. Daily vocabulary research describes **general communication**, not every field.

**Sources:** [CIS vocabulary article](https://cisedu.com/en-gb/world-of-cis/articles-education/how-many-english-words-you-must-know/) · Ogden’s own “+150 field words” extension

---

## A10 — Word family vs word form (why counts differ)

**Simple words:** Researchers count **word families** (run, ran, running = one family), not every separate form. That is why “850 words” and “27,000 words” can both be true — they are counting different units.

**Fundamental truth:** A “word” is not one fixed thing in linguistics. Inflections, compounds, and multi-word phrases change the total. Comparisons only make sense when the **unit of count** matches. Most daily-life thresholds use **families** or **lemmas** (dictionary headwords).

**Sources:** [Brysbaert et al. (2016)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full) · [Nation (2006)](https://lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)

# Part B — AI answer vs human answer

---

## B1 — AI picks likely words; humans pick meaningful words

**Simple words:** AI is built to choose the **most statistically likely** next word. Humans choose words for **rhythm, memory, humour, who they’re talking to, and what they care about**.

**Fundamental truth:** Prediction and intention are different engines. Likely words cluster into safe, common, “helpful” patterns. Human choices include surprises — unusual verbs, inside references, deliberate bluntness. That difference is measurable as **perplexity** (how predictable the word sequence is). AI text tends to be more predictable; human text less so.

**Sources:** [Pangram — perplexity & burstiness](https://www.pangram.com/blog/why-perplexity-and-burstiness-fail-to-detect-ai) · [Systematic review — AI detection](https://doi.org/10.55145/ajest.2026.05.01.012)

---

## B2 — Burstiness: humans vary sentence length; AI stays even

**Simple words:** Human writing jumps — short sentence. Then a longer one. AI often keeps sentences similar length and shape, like a steady beat.

**Fundamental truth:** **Burstiness** is variation in sentence length and structure over a passage. Effective human writing uses rhythm for emphasis. AI defaults to medium, parallel sentences because that pattern is common in its training data. Even rhythm feels “written by a machine” even when grammar is perfect.

**Sources:** [Quillbot — burstiness & perplexity](https://quillbot.com/blog/ai-writing-tools/burstiness-and-perplexity/)

---

## B3 — AI reuses the same “style words” in clusters

**Simple words:** Words like **delve, underscore, crucial, landscape, ensuring, highlights** appear far more often in AI text than in human text — especially **together**.

**Fundamental truth:** LLMs share training data and tuning goals (“sound professional, helpful, complete”). That produces a **shared dialect** of prestige and filler words. Kobak et al. showed this at scale: after ChatGPT, hundreds of **style words** (not topic words) spiked in millions of medical abstracts. One such word means little; **three or more in one short passage** is a strong signal.

**Sources:** [Science Advances — Kobak et al. (2025)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813)

---

## B4 — AI loves sentence templates

**Simple words:** Patterns like **“It’s not X, it’s Y,”** **“Not just X but also Y,”** rule-of-three lists, and **“In conclusion…”** show up far more in AI text.

**Fundamental truth:** Templates are high-probability structures — they satisfy “sounds complete” without new thought. **Negative parallelism** (“not X, it’s Y”) in particular exploded in AI and corporate writing (Atlantic, 2026). Humans use these devices too (speeches, ads), but AI overuses them because they are safe rhetorical shapes. Repeated templates in one piece = likely AI-assist.

**Sources:** [Atlantic — negative parallelism (2026)](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/07/ai-chatbot-writing-tic-negative-parallelism/687892/)

---

## B5 — AI coordinates and lists; humans subordinate and nuance

**Simple words:** AI joins ideas with **“and… and… and…”** and parallel phrases. Humans more often use **because, although, if** — nesting one idea inside another.

**Fundamental truth:** Coordination is syntactically simple and reads smoothly. Subordination carries logical relationships (cause, exception, condition) but is harder to generate consistently. Research on GPT-4o vs humans found AI uses **phrasal coordination ~2× more** and **participial tails** (“…, highlighting the importance of…”) **~5× more**. AI favours additive lists; humans favour layered argument.

**Sources:** [Do LLMs write like humans? (arXiv 2024)](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2410.16107) · [PLOS One — ChatGPT essays (2025)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0329410)

---

## B6 — AI is vague; humans (when good) are concrete

**Simple words:** AI says **“Experts believe”** or **“Studies show”** without naming anyone. A human expert usually gives **a name, a number, a date, or a failure they saw**.

**Fundamental truth:** Specifics require knowledge or experience. AI is optimised to **sound authoritative** even when detail is thin. Vague authority fills space without risk. Human expert writing (and human-feeling copy) anchors claims in **checkable or lived particulars**. Missing specifics where they belong is one of the strongest content-level tells.

**Sources:** [Atlantic — AI feeling (2026)](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/05/how-to-tell-ai-writing/687345/)

---

## B7 — AI balances both sides; humans take a side

**Simple words:** AI often gives **“While X has benefits, it also has challenges”** even when one clear answer would help more.

**Fundamental truth:** RLHF (training to be helpful and harmless) pushes models toward **non-offensive completeness**. That produces synthetic balance — every topic gets pros and cons, importance inflated equally. Humans writing to inform usually **select** — this matters more than that; for your question, here is the answer. Selectivity feels human; universal balance feels AI.

**Sources:** [Guardian — AI-ese (2024)](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2024/apr/16/techscape-ai-gadgest-humane-ai-pin-chatgpt)

---

## B8 — Concept collision: AI mixes metaphors that don’t fit

**Simple words:** AI sometimes combines images that sound fine but **don’t belong in the same picture** — e.g. “nestled amid the war” or “overarching pillars that undergird.”

**Fundamental truth:** LLMs choose words by **co-occurrence statistics**, not by holding one mental model. Two phrases can each be common in training data but **incoherent together**. Humans usually stay inside one frame per sentence because meaning is grounded in a single imagined scene. Fluent nonsense is a deep tell — grammar passes, concept fails.

**Sources:** [Atlantic — how to tell AI writing (2026)](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/05/how-to-tell-ai-writing/687345/)

---

## B9 — AI has a “customer service voice”

**Simple words:** AI sounds **polished, agreeable, and complete** — like a helpful textbook or chatbot. It rarely says “I don’t know” or skips what isn’t relevant to you.

**Fundamental truth:** The default LLM persona is **service**: cover the question, avoid friction, end helpfully. That produces flat helpfulness — no struggle, doubt, or odd preference. Human responses are **addressed**: they answer *your* question, leave things out on purpose, and carry tone (warm, blunt, tired, excited). Personality is selective, not uniformly pleasant.

**Sources:** [Nature — stylometry AI vs human (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05986-3)

---

## B10 — AI text is uniform; human text is messier across writers

**Simple words:** Different AI models produce **similar, tight clusters** of style. Human writers spread across a **wider range** — formal, casual, clumsy, brilliant.

**Fundamental truth:** LLMs converge on a shared “machine register” despite different brands. Stylometry studies show human-authored texts form **broader, more heterogeneous** clusters; LLM outputs cluster **by model** with high internal similarity. Uniform off-ness — tone, structure, and argument all slightly wrong the same way — feels AI even when you cannot name one tell.

**Sources:** [Nature — stylometry (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05986-3) · [EMNLP — linguistic profiling (2025)](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1163/)

---

## B11 — Most humans cannot spot AI writing reliably

**Simple words:** Average people guess AI vs human at about **50–59%** accuracy — barely better than a coin flip. Trained tools do much better (~85–96%).

**Fundamental truth:** Fluency fooled us. Modern AI grammar is clean; surface reading no longer separates the classes. Humans rely on weak cues (“sounds formal,” “too smooth”) that misfire on careful human writers and non-native English. Detection works better at **population scale** (pattern statistics) than **individual intuition**. Do not trust gut alone; use pattern clusters or tools — with known false-positive limits.

**Sources:** [TURING — LREC 2026](http://www.lrec-conf.org/proceedings/lrec2026/pdf/2026.lrec2026-1.355.pdf) · [JudgeGPT study (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3795513.3807431) · [Cambridge — Bot or not (2026)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/45E6DC0BB90AA648654D5AE243F6C667/S1930297526100424a.pdf/bot-or-not-can-people-tell-the-difference-between-stories-written-by-a-human-or-by-an-ai-system.pdf)

---

## B12 — One pattern never proves AI

**Simple words:** One em dash, one “delve,” or one “Additionally” does **not** mean AI wrote it. **Clusters** matter.

**Fundamental truth:** Authorship is probabilistic. Formal humans, legal boilerplate, ESL writers, and style guides mimic many AI tells. Accusation from a single feature causes false positives (detectors wrongly flag ~61% of ESL writing in one Stanford-cited study). The stack rule: **2+ pattern categories** or **3+ style words** or **chatbot residue** → treat as likely AI-assist. Never “100% AI” from style alone.

**Sources:** [Pangram — detector limits](https://www.pangram.com/blog/why-perplexity-and-burstiness-fail-to-detect-ai)

---

## B13 — What makes copy feel human (action list)

**Simple words:** Use **plain verbs**, **one real detail**, **uneven sentence length**, **a clear point of view**, and **no empty opener or recap**.

**Fundamental truth:** Human-feeling is not sloppiness — it is **grounded selectivity**. The reader should sense someone chose what to say. Concrete anchors (number, name, limit) prove attention. Rhythm variation proves a human ear. Cutting templates proves the writer did not default to “complete essay mode.” These edits work because they reverse the statistical defaults LLMs optimise for.

---

### Human vs AI — at a glance

| Feels like AI | Feels like a human responded |
|---------------|------------------------------|
| Smooth, even, complete | Uneven, selective, pointed |
| Style words in clusters (delve, crucial, ensuring…) | Plain verbs + topic-specific words |
| Templates (“not X but Y,” rule of three) | Direct claim, once |
| “Experts say” without names | Named source, number, or limit |
| Both sides equally weighted | Clear take for this reader |
| Textbook-helper tone | Someone talking to *you* |
| Metaphors that don’t quite fit | One clear picture per idea |
| “In conclusion…” / “I hope this helps” | Stops when the point is made |

# Part C — How this connects to Shroffin guide writing

---

## C1 — High-frequency words + human voice

**Simple words:** Guides should use **common words** (low cognitive load) but still sound **human** — specific, direct, not template-smooth.

**Fundamental truth:** Plain language and anti-AI voice pull the same direction for different reasons. Plain language reduces **extraneous load** (hard decoding). Human voice adds **grounding** (specifics, selectivity). AI can write simply and still feel AI if it is vague, templated, and evenly rhythmic. Good guide copy is **simple + concrete + addressed to the reader’s situation**.

---

## C2 — Specialist terms are allowed when they earn their place

**Simple words:** You do not need to stay inside 850 or 1,000 words for a home-loan guide. Use **plain words by default**; use **precise terms** (EMI, deductible, lien) when the reader needs the real label.

**Fundamental truth:** Ogden and Munroe prove paraphrase is possible; Nation and Zipf prove frequency matters. For financial guides, the balance is: **default to high-frequency explanation**, introduce the official term once, then use it where precision saves sentences. Hiding necessary terms creates ambiguity; showing off rare terms creates load.
