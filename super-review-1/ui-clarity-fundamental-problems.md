# UI clarity — fundamental problems

Each item is a reusable defect pattern. The two sentences state the core. The five sentences explain what is really going wrong at a basic level. Nothing here is tied to a specific screen or product.

---

## A1 — Fading a path that still matters

When two inputs both decide the outcome, dimming one teaches “this one doesn’t matter.” Equal causes must look equally real until the result is chosen.

The real problem is a false importance signal. The interface treats “not the binding answer” as if it were “not part of the decision.” Users read visual weakness as “ignore this.” That breaks trust in the logic, because they cannot see how both sides earned the final figure. Clarity requires both sides to stay fully readable; only the outcome should be marked as chosen.

---

## A2 — Layout that steals width from meaning

Layout that steals width forces meaning into tall, broken lines, so reading feels like work. Space should serve the words and numbers the user must understand, not the decoration of order.

The real problem is structure competing with comprehension. Fixed columns, gutters, and labels take room that the actual content needed. Text wraps early, lines get short, and the stack grows long. Length then feels like complexity even when the idea is simple. The user leaves tired, not informed.

---

## A3 — Restating the final answer

Repeating the same conclusion after the user already saw it adds length without new meaning. Once the story reaches the answer, stop — don’t re-narrate it.

The real problem is duplication mistaken for clarity. Saying the result again does not teach; it only asks the brain to check whether anything changed. That check is unpaid mental work. Extra restatement also makes the walk feel unfinished or padded. One earned ending is enough.

---

## A4 — Chrome with no job

Visual marks that don’t change what someone understands are noise. Every mark should earn its place by teaching or guiding; otherwise remove it.

The real problem is decoration posing as guidance. Borders, rails, and accents draw the eye without adding a rule or a next step. Attention is limited; wasted attention feels like clutter. Clutter reads as “this product is complicated.” Remove what does not teach.

---

## 1 — Highlighting only the winner among equal causes

Highlighting only the winner while both paths are required invents a false hierarchy. Show how both contribute; mark the outcome, not “ignore the other half.”

The real problem is confusing “which value won” with “which path mattered.” In a min-or-max style decision, every path was required to produce the set of candidates. Calling out only the winner teaches people to dismiss the rest. Later they cannot explain the result to themselves or to someone else. Mark the comparison result; keep every contributing path fully present.

---

## 2 — Parallel parts framed as separate stories

Parallel parts of one decision framed as two separate lists feel like two products. One decision needs one continuous story with a single ending.

The real problem is broken narrative unity. Restarted numbering, separate headers, and disconnected blocks make the brain open two files instead of one. Users never feel a single arc from inputs to answer. Without that arc, they cannot hold the philosophy of the calculation. One decision deserves one path that ends once.

---

## 3 — The same figure as more than one “final”

Putting the same figure in more than one “final” place makes people wonder which is real. One primary answer; everything else is setup, not a second finale.

The real problem is competing finales. Each “big” number claims to be the destination. The user then compares them instead of understanding how they were earned. That comparison is anxiety, not learning. Authority belongs to a single primary answer; prior numbers are steps, not rivals.

---

## 4 — Full explanation under every step

Explaining every line in full while the math already shows it doubles the load. Default to short; put depth behind a choice to open more.

The real problem is teaching every detail at the default level. Parallel text under every step forces reading twice: once for the figure, once for the essay. Most people came for the chain, not a manual. Forced depth turns a short walk into a long scroll. Offer more only when someone asks for more.

---

## 5 — Showing steps that change nothing

Steps that change nothing still look like work and can feel like an error. Hide or collapse work that doesn’t move the number.

The real problem is noise from zero-effect work. Empty operations still take space, attention, and doubt (“did something fail?”). Doubt is expensive; it makes people re-check inputs they already trust. Showing only steps that move the result keeps the story honest and short. Absence of a step can mean “nothing to apply,” which is clearer than a zero line.

---

## 6 — Uneven visual weight for equal ideas

If two ideas are equally important, one looking much bigger or smaller lies about importance. Visual weight should match true weight.

The real problem is layout lying about priority. Length, contrast, and placement are read as “this matters more.” When that signal disagrees with the real rule, users learn the wrong model. Wrong models survive even after they read the correct sentence. Match size and emphasis to true role, not to how many lines a block happens to have.

---

## 7 — A bad shared pattern reused everywhere

A bad layout pattern copied into every drawer multiplies confusion site-wide. Fix the shared pattern once; don’t patch each screen alone.

The real problem is systemic repetition of a local flaw. One cramped or noisy template becomes the house style for “how we explain numbers.” Each new surface then inherits the same exit risk. Users meet the same friction and conclude the whole product is hard. Repair the shared building block; do not treat each instance as a one-off.

---

## 8 — Insider words as the first words

Leading with insider words tells ordinary people “this isn’t for you.” Plain meaning first; technical names second or on demand.

The real problem is gatekeeping by vocabulary. The first phrase sets belonging. If it belongs to specialists, newcomers stop before the math. Stopping is not laziness; it is a rational response to exclusion. Meaning in everyday language invites them in; jargon can follow once the idea is held.

---

## 9 — Homework before the answer they asked for

Forcing a side calculation before the thing they asked for feels like a test. Lead with the answer they came for; show the side step only if it unlocks understanding.

The real problem is inverted priority. The user’s question defines what “first” means. Anything that delays that answer feels like unpaid schoolwork. Even correct side steps can cause exit if they come too early. Put the sought result first; supporting conversions second, and only when they help.

---

## 10 — Dumping a secondary system by default

Opening a full secondary system when they asked one question overwhelms. Offer depth as optional, not as the default wall.

The real problem is scope explosion. One question deserves one answer layer. A second full system (tables, schedules, deep lists) arrives as a new product inside the first. Cognitive budget runs out; the original question is forgotten. Depth should be a door someone chooses to open, not a wall they must climb.

---

## 11 — One fact in two unit languages

One fact shown in two unit systems makes people hunt for a mistake. One fact, one unit language everywhere it appears.

The real problem is forced translation work. The brain treats mismatched units as possible error until proven otherwise. That proof costs time and trust. Trust lost on units spills onto the whole calculation. Pick one clear language for a fact and keep it consistent across surfaces.

---

## 12 — Stacked signals that say the same thing

Several cues saying the same thing don’t clarify; they compete. One clear signal beats three overlapping ones.

The real problem is redundant emphasis. Fade, rails, badges, and labels all shouting “look here” cancel each other. The eye does not know which cue is the rule. Competing cues feel busy, then confusing. Choose one honest signal for one job.

---

## 13 — Too many section breaks in one short story

Heavy dividers turn one short story into many “chapters,” so the brain never feels finished. Structure should feel like one path, not a stack of cards.

The real problem is false segmentation. Each hard break asks the mind to close one topic and open another. On a short walk, that is too many closings. The user feels they are still “in the middle” even near the end. Soften structure so one decision reads as one continuous path.

---

## One line under all of them

At base level, the product is making the user do extra mental work — to rank what matters, decode layout, re-read duplicates, or translate jargon — instead of simply seeing how the number was earned.
