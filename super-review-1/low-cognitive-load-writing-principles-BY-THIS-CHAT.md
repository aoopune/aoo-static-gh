# Low cognitive-load writing principles

**Purpose:** Principles for writing text so readers spend less brain power decoding structure, and more on meaning. Distilled from research and plain-language sources on working memory, sentence length, one-idea packing, and reader expectations.

**How to use:** Before publishing copy, check against the **One-line principles** list. When a line fails, open that source’s **Fundamental** for why. Prefer meaning unchanged with less mental work — never “dumb down” the idea; clear the path to it.

**Location:** `super-review-1/low-cognitive-load-writing-principles.md`

---

## Quick stack (all sources, one pass)

1. One idea per sentence; one topic per paragraph.
2. Aim ~15–20 words average; split past ~25 when meaning allows.
3. Put old/context first, new point last; keep subject next to verb.
4. Cut words that do not change meaning; prefer short, familiar words and active voice.
5. Do not force the reader to hold many unfinished people, clauses, or conditions at once.
6. Link sentences so the mind does not “switch rooms” without a bridge.

## Sources → principles

### 1. Clear and short (Digital.gov / Plain Language)

**Source:** https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short

**One-line:** Put only one idea in each sentence, and break long clause-stacks into separate short sentences the brain can finish one at a time.

**Fundamental:**
A sentence is a processing unit: if it carries several ideas, exceptions, and side clauses at once, the reader must juggle them before the main point lands, and often loses it. Short sentences do not make hard topics small — they cut the hard topic into pieces the mind can finish, store, and then connect. Dependent clauses and packed “everything in one breath” sentences confuse because they hide which statement is primary. The same rule scales up: one topic per paragraph, short sections with clear headings, so the reader never faces a dense wall that costs energy just to enter.

### 2. Be concise (18F)

**Source:** https://guides.18f.gov/content-guide/our-approach/be-concise/

**One-line:** Say the thing once, in short sentences (check anything over ~25 words), without padding, caveats-as-grammar, or verb-to-noun fog.

**Fundamental:**
Concision is not coldness — it is precision: every extra hedge, softener, and abstract noun (`-ion` / `-ment`) forces an extra decode step before the reader knows what to do. One idea per sentence, and one clear action where action is the point, stops the reader from hunting for the ask inside a winding clause. Caveats should not warp the whole sentence into “you may be able to…” when “you can” is true enough for the job. Rewrite long government-style sentences into two plain ones that still keep the relationship (cause, condition, result) without one overloaded line.

### 3. Cognitive Load Theory for writers (ReadabilityFormulas)

**Source:** https://readabilityformulas.com/improve-your-writing-style-with-cognitive-load-theory/

**One-line:** Working memory is small — cut needless complexity in how you present the idea so the reader’s limited attention can build understanding instead of fighting the prose.

**Fundamental:**
Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller) says the mind holds only a little new information at once; overload that buffer and understanding, memory, and action all drop. Load comes in three kinds: **intrinsic** (how hard the topic itself is), **extraneous** (how badly the writing is designed — jargon, mess, long fog), and **germane** (useful effort that builds a lasting mental model). Good writing cannot remove intrinsic difficulty of a hard subject, but it must ruthlessly cut extraneous load and gently support germane load (examples, order, links to what the reader already knows). Even a short sentence can trigger many mental steps (decode words → parse structure → infer action → remember); writers who ignore that tax will exhaust readers before the message sticks.

### 4. Ideal sentence length for readability

**Source:** https://toolsforwriting.com/blog/ideal-sentence-length-for-readability

**One-line:** Keep document average around 15–20 words; past ~25 comprehension falls because the sentence outruns short-term memory.

**Fundamental:**
Readers hold a sentence in short-term memory while they assemble meaning; when the sentence is too long, that buffer overflows and the thread is lost before the period. Data often cited from press-readability work: very short averages approach full comprehension; around 14 words still strong; past 25 words understanding drops hard; extreme averages can collapse comprehension near floor. “Average” matters — mix short and long for rhythm, but keep the mean in the sweet band so the whole page feels holdable. Lead with the point, then qualify; front-loading hedges and conditions makes the reader store noise before they know what the sentence is about.

### 5. Clear sentences the Robert Gunning way

**Source:** https://readabilityformulas.com/writing-clearer-sentences-the-robert-gunning-way/

**One-line:** Write to express, not impress — “fog” is complexity that serves the writer’s display, not the reader’s grasp.

**Fundamental:**
Gunning’s fog idea is that unclear prose is often vanity or habit: long words and long sentences that raise the cost of reading without raising the value of the idea. Shorter sentences and plainer words reduce cognitive load and free working memory for meaning. Rhythm still matters: all short can feel choppy, all long can bury the reader — aim for a readable average and vary length. The golden rule underneath the formula is ethical as much as stylistic: the purpose of the text is the reader’s understanding, not the writer’s showcase.

### 6. Plain language to minimize cognitive load (Cheung / IEEE)

**Source:** https://doi.org/10.1109/tpc.2017.2759639

**One-line:** Plain language is not style preference — it is cutting extraneous mental work so limited working memory can actually take the message in.

**Fundamental:**
Working memory holds only about four or five items for a few seconds; if the reading task plus life stress overflows that, learning and comprehension fail. Plain-language moves (active voice, verbs instead of heavy noun forms, chunks, one idea per sentence) work mainly by lowering **extraneous** load — the avoidable tax of bad wording and structure. When language is plain enough, more of the message can be taken in with fast, automatic thinking instead of slow, effortful decoding. The paper also frames this as fairness: people already carrying heavier mental burden have less spare capacity, so unclear writing locks them out of understanding that clearer writing would have given them.

### 7. Minimized Text Complexity Guidelines (UNC)

**Source:** https://www.med.unc.edu/healthsciences/clds/wp-content/uploads/sites/859/2021/06/Minimized-Text-Complexity-Guidelines-version-2.03.2021.pdf

**One-line:** For hard-to-reach readers, go beyond “plain” — one key point per sentence, very short lines, high cohesion, almost no negation or density tricks.

**Fundamental:**
Plain language is necessary but not always enough when readers sit at beginning literacy levels; complexity must be actively minimized at document, paragraph, and sentence scale. Sentence-level rules go strict: one key point (and one main clause unit) per sentence, often targeting roughly 8–10 words, with complex sentences only when a real causal/relational idea needs them. Avoid negation and soft “almost/hardly” phrasing that force the brain to reverse a meaning before it can store it. Maximize cohesion (words and structure that keep the same thread alive) so the reader is not rebuilding “who/what we are talking about” from scratch every line.

### 8. Three cognitive principles for writing clarity (AJE)

**Source:** https://www.aje.com/arc/improve-your-writing-clarity-through-these-three-cognitive-learning-principles

**One-line:** One idea per sentence, delete words that do not change meaning, and place information where readers expect it (topic early, stress late).

**Fundamental:**
Clarity fails when the writer overloads working memory, assumes the reader already knows what the writer knows, or fights the order readers use to take in English prose. Cognitive load is managed first by one idea per sentence and by cutting any word that can leave without changing meaning. Reader expectations are managed by putting the known topic early (so the mind knows what the sentence is about) and the new, important claim late (where English readers naturally emphasize). Linking words (`however`, `therefore`, and so on) and end-to-start bridges between sentences reduce the “where did we go?” cost of moving to the next line.

### 9. The Science of Scientific Writing (Gopen & Swan)

**Source:** https://www.americanscientist.org/blog/the-long-view/the-science-of-scientific-writing

**One-line:** Readers interpret by structure — put information where they expect it, or they spend energy untangling form instead of grasping content.

**Fundamental:**
Hard reading is often not only hard ideas; it is good ideas delivered against the grain of how English readers search a sentence. Readers expect the subject and verb close together; long interruptions between them feel like digressions and force the mind to hold an unfinished “who?” until the verb arrives. They expect the opening of a sentence (topic position) to give context and linkage backward, and the end (stress position) to deliver the new point worth emphasis. When writers violate these locations again and again, readers divert power from understanding the science (or any content) to decoding the architecture — and misreading rises fast as context gets denser.

### 10. Reader expectation research (Gopen)

**Source:** https://georgegopen.com/reader-expectation-research/

**One-line:** Place context, emphasis, and connection where English readers already look — clarity is alignment with those habits, not decoration.

**Fundamental:**
Independent studies across fields keep confirming the same structural claim: people retain and process prose better when content sits in the slots their reading habits already reserve for context, emphasis, and forward link. The 1990 Gopen & Swan framework is not a taste rule; it is a map of those slots. Writers who learn the map can keep full technical meaning while raising the odds that most readers hear the intended emphasis. Improving structure this way often improves the thought itself, because unclear placement usually means the writer has not yet decided what is context and what is the point.

### 11. Writing and Cognitive Load Theory (Natalie Wexler)

**Source:** https://researched.org.uk/2019/06/24/writing-and-cognitive-load-theory/

**One-line:** Anything not already stored in long-term memory eats working memory — so unfamiliar content plus tangled sentences double-taxes the reader (and the writer).

**Fundamental:**
Working memory is consciousness with a tiny desk: only a few new things, briefly. Reading is easier when topic knowledge and word patterns already live in long-term memory, because then decoding and sense-making do not compete for the same scarce slots. Writing is harder still: spelling, word choice, sentence shape, organization, and content all fight for that desk at once. The practical truth for our use: if the *reader* must learn new facts and also fight long, overloaded sentences, comprehension collapses — so move as much as possible out of “juggle now” (clear structure, known words, earned complexity) and into “already know / easy to finish.”

### 12. Does splitting make a sentence easier? (Frontiers, 2023)

**Source:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2023.1208451/full

**One-line:** Splitting a hard sentence usually helps readability — and two clear sentences often beat chopping into many fragments.

**Fundamental:**
Text simplification often assumes “more splits = easier,” but human ratings show splitting helps mainly when it truly reduces processing burden, not when it shreds meaning into too many pieces. Bisecting (two sentences) tends to improve readability more reliably than three-or-more splits, which can feel choppy or informationally awkward. Grammar, fluency, and preserved meaning still matter; a mechanically split sentence that breaks sense does not win. For writing practice: when one sentence is doing two jobs, prefer one clean cut into two full sentences over a hail of tiny shards.

### 13. Dependency Locality Theory (Gibson)

**Sources:**  
https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_2000_DLT.pdf  
https://medium.com/@riazleghari/ted-gibson-on-the-cognitive-architecture-of-language-e0ab8b422480

**One-line:** Sentence difficulty rises with how much unfinished structure you must hold and how far the mind must reach back to connect a new word to its partner.

**Fundamental:**
As we read left to right, the brain pays two costs: **storage** (keeping incomplete expectations — e.g. a subject still waiting for its verb) and **integration** (attaching the current word to the right earlier piece). Integration gets more expensive the more other people, things, and clauses sit between the two pieces that belong together. So a sentence that introduces many new subjects/referents, or stretches subject far from verb, is not merely “long” — it is computationally heavy. The writing fix that follows from the science: keep dependencies local, introduce fewer new entities per sentence, and resolve what you open before opening three more doors.

---

### 14. Omit needless words / sentence length perception (PLOS One)

**Source:** https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282146

**One-line:** Shorter sentences aid readability — and when editing, cut words that add length without adding meaning (“omit needless words”).

**Fundamental:**
Readability research treats sentence length as a real driver of how inclusive and graspable a text is for broad audiences. Adults can often sense “this sentence is long” quickly from the span of the line, which makes length a practical editing alarm, not only a formula output. The classic remedy is not clever synonyms — it is deletion of words that do no semantic work. For our principles file: if meaning survives the cut, the cut was load the reader should never have paid.

---

### 15. Principles of Clear Writing (U.S. National Archives)

**Source:** https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/write/legal-docs/clear-writing.html

**One-line:** Prefer simple, active, affirmative, declarative sentences — state one thing per sentence, then list conditions instead of nesting them.

**Fundamental:**
The more a sentence leaves the simple “who did what” shape, the harder it becomes to recover the intended rule or fact. Legal and policy prose fails most often by packing conditions, exceptions, and subordinates into one run-on unit until meaning is ambiguous. The durable fix is structural: one statement per sentence; strip modifiers that do not change the rule; put simple conditions first or, when conditions are many/complex, state the rule first and then the conditions (often as a list). Clarity here is operational: the reader must be able to apply the sentence, not merely admire it.

## Master principle list (copy-ready)

Use these as the standing rules when writing or revising text:

| ID | One-line principle |
|----|--------------------|
| P1 | One idea per sentence; one topic per paragraph. |
| P2 | Average ~15–20 words; inspect and usually split past ~25. |
| P3 | If a word can leave without changing meaning, cut it. |
| P4 | Prefer active, affirmative, everyday verbs over noun-stacks and hedges. |
| P5 | Subject and verb stay close; do not bury the verb behind a long interruption. |
| P6 | Old/context first; new point last; bridge sentence-to-sentence. |
| P7 | Few new people/things per sentence; finish one dependency before opening many. |
| P8 | Hard ideas may stay hard — writing must not add a second hard layer of structure. |
| P9 | Prefer one clean split into two sentences over many tiny fragments. |
| P10 | Write to express for the reader’s limited working memory, not to impress. |
